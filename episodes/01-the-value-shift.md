## 1. The engineering problem (~2:30)

[VISUAL: Title card — *Episode 1: The Value Shift*]

I've been a senior software engineer for eighteen years. I've survived more paradigm shifts than I can count — waterfall to agile, monoliths to microservices, on-prem to cloud, REST to GraphQL to whatever's next. Each one felt disorienting at the time and obvious in retrospect.

This one feels different.

[VISUAL: simple split — "2008" with an old IDE / "2026" with an AI chat panel]

AI didn't just change the tools. It changed the bottleneck. For eighteen years, the bottleneck in software was *execution* — could you write the code, debug the code, ship the code fast enough? That scarcity was what made my skills valuable. I was good at execution. I'm still good at execution.

But execution isn't scarce anymore.

If you've been doing this work long enough to develop the muscle memory of *being the one who builds*, you've probably felt what I'm about to describe. I'll just say it plainly.

[VISUAL: hold on text — "I feel insignificant sometimes."]

I feel insignificant sometimes.

I can direct AI's work. I can set architecture. I can catch the ten percent that AI gets wrong — and that ten percent matters, because it's where the bugs live and the security holes hide. But the *act* of writing code, the thing that made me feel like a craftsman for almost two decades, isn't the primary thing anymore. Some days I'm a reviewer more than a creator.

A senior developer in a Reddit thread last year put it this way: *I haven't written a single line of code in four months.* Juggling AI tabs and YouTube videos while models work. Missing the slower, careful architecture work that used to be the job.

That's not a story about laziness. It's a story about a job changing shape underneath you.

The engineering problem this episode is about isn't a technical bug. It's a much harder one:

**When the bottleneck moves, what made you valuable last year doesn't make you valuable next year. And nobody sends a memo.**

## 2. What the industry says (~2:30)

The industry has a name for this.

[VISUAL: Quote card — Trejo with link]

Tony Trejo, in his *Value Shift Framework*, says it cleanly:

> "AI didn't replace engineers. It replaced execution as the bottleneck. And when the bottleneck moves, value moves with it."

[VISUAL: Old / New scarcity table — animate the column shift]

What was scarce before AI is no longer scarce. What's scarce now is different. Knowing *what* to build. Architectural decisions that survive contact with reality. Verifying AI output against production constraints. Orchestrating human-plus-AI teams.

Ivan Turkovic put it more bluntly. The title of his post: *AI Made Writing Code Easier. It Made Engineering Harder.*

Milan Jovanović compressed the whole thing to one line that I keep coming back to:

[VISUAL: Quote card — Jovanović]

> "The skill isn't prompting. It's owning correctness."

That's the industry's read. And it's right, as far as it goes. The industry has organized this into four disciplines — prompt craft, context engineering, intent engineering, spec engineering. There are courses, frameworks, conference talks. Everyone is teaching the new skills.

But here's what I noticed once I started actually living inside the new work, not just reading about it. The industry can name what changed. The industry can't quite name what's *durable*. It can tell you to invest in judgment instead of execution. It can't tell you what judgment is made of, or how to grow more of it, or what survives when this technology becomes the next thing.

For that question, I had to look somewhere else.

## 3. What actually happened (~4:00)

[VISUAL: shift to a quieter, one-on-one feel]

Let me tell you what was actually going on for me when I started working through this.

It was March of this year. I'd been using AI tools heavily for about a year on a personal project — a scripture study system. MCP servers, a publishing pipeline, deep research workflows, a companion app for tracking practice. Real work. Useful work. But I was also feeling the pattern I just described — the reviewer-not-creator drift, the prompt spiral, the comparison to other engineers' highlight reels.

So I sat down to write a study about my own situation. Just for myself. Titled "Staying Relevant." Eighteen years in, doing AI-assisted work, asking honestly: what do I actually bring?

[VISUAL: cpuchip.net study page — "Staying Relevant" — scroll slowly through]

The study took a few hours. I did the research with AI's help — pulled in articles, ran cross-references, structured the argument. The AI didn't write the study. I did. But it executed the parts I directed.

And when I finished, I realized something that the writing itself was the evidence of:

[VISUAL: hold on text]

The thing the AI helped me do, I couldn't have done alone in the time I had. And the thing I directed it to do, it couldn't have done alone at all.

That sentence is the whole episode, if you only remember one.

The judgment about what to write — mine. The taste about which sources mattered — mine. The personal honesty that made the piece worth writing — that has to come from a person; AI doesn't have anything to be vulnerable about. The cross-reference to scripture, the framing through the parable of the talents — those came out of years of study, not out of a model.

But the execution speed. The ability to pull together five articles and synthesize them in an hour instead of a day. The willingness to do another draft when the first wasn't quite right. That came from the partnership.

Now, the failure I have to include, because the people I'm talking to deserve the honest version.

[VISUAL: a different tone — failure beat, plain]

I've also had weeks where I was just yelling at the model. Prompt spiral after prompt spiral. Three hours trying to get it to understand my database schema before I gave up and wrote the migration myself in twenty minutes. Days where I felt like a janitor cleaning up after a confident toddler who never sleeps.

The partnership doesn't always work. Sometimes the value the industry promises shows up. Sometimes you're just tired and the model is producing slop and you can't tell the difference between the two.

But across a year — across MCP servers I built that actually work, study documents I'm proud of, a publishing pipeline that ships real content — there's enough signal under the noise to be sure of something. The value shifted. And the version of me that's adapting to the shift is doing more useful work than the version of me that just wrote code fast.

That isn't an industry argument. It's a personal one. The industry got me to the doorway. What happened after I walked through it is what the rest of this episode is about.

## 4. The pattern underneath (~4:00)

The industry's framing stops at "the bottleneck moved, here are the new skills." It can't tell you *why* judgment is the durable thing. Or what judgment is even made of. Or how to invest in the right kind of it.

For those questions, I had to go back to scripture.

[VISUAL: Scripture card — D&C 130:18-19, the words large]

> "Whatever principle of intelligence we attain unto in this life, it will rise with us in the resurrection. And if a person gains more knowledge and intelligence in this life through his diligence and obedience than another, he will have so much the advantage in the world to come."
> — Doctrine and Covenants 130:18-19

Read that with the engineering problem in mind. The intelligence isn't the code. The intelligence is the *judgment* — the discernment, the design instinct, the ability to see what matters and act on it. *That's* what rises with you. AI doesn't have a resurrection. You do.

When the scripture says *whatever principle of intelligence we attain*, it doesn't mean facts memorized or commands run. It means the kind of seeing that comes from working through hard problems honestly. The kind that takes years. The kind that gets stronger when you struggle, not when you skip the struggle.

There's a deeper pattern, too.

[VISUAL: Abraham 4-5 reference — the words "organized and watched"]

In Abraham 4 and 5, when the Gods organized the heavens and earth, they didn't execute alone — they counseled. They ordered. They watched things "until they obeyed." Their work was not muscular. It was *directional*. Intelligence applied to chaos, with patience, with judgment about what to do next, with attention to whether the thing they had organized was actually obeying.

That's the work I keep landing back in when I'm using AI well. Not typing. *Organizing*. Watching what emerges. Course-correcting when something drifts. Knowing when something's done. That's not a new skill the industry just invented this year. It's the oldest pattern in scripture. And now it's also our daily work.

There's a question the episode has to ask, because the alternative is despair.

[VISUAL: Parable of the Talents card]

In the parable of the talents, the master gives different servants different amounts. Five talents, two, one. When he returns, he doesn't ask "did you produce the most?" He asks, "did you *use* what you were given?"

The one with five made five more. The one with two made two more. Both are told the same thing: *Well done, thou good and faithful servant.*

The servant who was condemned wasn't the one with the least. It was the one who buried what he was given.

Eighteen years of engineering is a talent. So is whatever you've been given — years of experience, taste for good design, instinct for what will fail in production, agency, the ability to care whether the work matters. AI can't take any of that away from you. It can only commoditize the *previous version* of what you did with it.

The question isn't "am I still relevant?" The question is "am I using what I've been given?"

That changes everything.

## 5. What this means for you (~1:30)

[VISUAL: practical close, plain]

Here's what I think you do with this, whether you're a senior engineer feeling the same pressure or just someone trying to figure out where AI fits.

**Stop measuring yourself by execution speed.** If you keep using the old metric, you'll feel inadequate forever — like a calculator operator comparing themselves to a spreadsheet. The job changed. Measure by decisions made, systems designed, problems correctly framed.

**Take inventory of what you actually bring.** Not what LinkedIn says you should bring. What you *actually* have. Years of debugging hard production issues. Domain understanding nobody on the internet has. The taste to tell good output from confident garbage. The agency to care whether the thing you build matters. Write that list down. It's longer than you think.

**Treat AI like a partner you direct, not a replacement that displaces.** You bring the judgment about what to build. It brings the execution. When that partnership works, you'll do more in a day than you used to do in a week — and the *kind* of work you do will be more valuable, not less.

**Invest in the durable layer.** Tools change. Problems don't. The thing rising with you in the resurrection isn't the framework you learned this year. It's the judgment you've been building since you started.

[VISUAL: closing card with episode 2 teaser]

That was Episode 1. The industry has named four disciplines for working with AI well — prompt craft, context engineering, intent engineering, spec engineering. They're real. They're useful. Next episode I'll walk through what each one is, what it gets right, and what they all miss together. Then in episode three we'll start getting to the seven things scripture says about creation that the industry hasn't gotten to yet.

Thanks for watching. If this resonated, the written version with all the sources is at cpuchip.net.
